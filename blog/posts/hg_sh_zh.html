Henyey-Greenstein in Spherical Harmonics

Zonal harmonics and how to rotate them

2026-07-30

1. Introduction

This post was prompted by a practical problem. While working on volumetric rendering I wanted a cheap way to light participating media with environment lighting stored as spherical harmonics (SH). Two well-known facts collided in my head:

  • SH is a famously convenient approximation of an irradiance map — Ramamoorthi and Hanrahan showed that diffuse irradiance of any environment map is captured almost entirely by just 9 SH coefficients (bands 0–2).
  • The Henyey-Greenstein (HG) function is the phase function used in volumetric rendering to describe anisotropic scattering.

So the question is: can we represent the HG lobe itself in SH, and then light a volume with a couple of dot products instead of any integration? Spoiler: yes, and the key insight is that HG is a zonal harmonic with beautifully simple coefficients.

Every interactive figure below can be switched between a 3D sphere view — drag to orbit, scroll to zoom, colored lines are the \(x\) (red), \(y\) (green), \(z\) (blue) axes — and a polar plot of the underlying Legendre-polynomial expansion. All \(g\) sliders are linked: change one and every figure updates. Colors and polar radii use a fixed absolute scale: full saturation / outer ring corresponds to the cap value adjustable in the bottom-right corner (default \(2\)).

2. Main part

2.1 What is HG

The Henyey-Greenstein phase function describes the probability density of light being scattered from direction \(\omega_i\) into direction \(\omega_o\). It depends only on the angle \(\theta\) between them, \(\cos\theta = \omega_i \cdot \omega_o\):

\[ p(\theta) = \frac{1 - g^2}{4\pi\,(1 + g^2 - 2g\cos\theta)^{3/2}} \]

The single parameter \(g \in (-1, 1)\) is the mean cosine of the scattering angle: \(g > 0\) gives forward scattering, \(g < 0\) backward scattering, and \(g = 0\) is isotropic. Drag the slider and watch the lobe stretch:

Henyey-Greenstein phase function

The lobe points along \(+z\). Positive values are red — HG is always non-negative. This \(g\) is shared with all other figures.

2.2 What are SH and ZH

Spherical harmonics \(Y_l^m(\omega)\) are an orthonormal basis of functions on the sphere, the analog of the Fourier basis on a circle. Any spherical function can be approximated by

\[ f(\omega) \approx \sum_{l=0}^{N}\sum_{m=-l}^{l} c_l^m\, Y_l^m(\omega), \qquad \int_{S^2} Y_i(\omega)\, Y_j(\omega)\, d\omega = \delta_{ij}. \]

The basis functions are organized in bands \(l = 0, 1, 2, \dots\), each band \(l\) containing \(2l+1\) functions. Here are all bands up to the third degree (\(l \le 2\), 9 functions, positive values red, negative blue):

The middle column \(m = 0\) is special: those functions depend only on the polar angle \(\theta\) and are rotationally symmetric about the \(z\)-axis. They are called zonal harmonics (ZH), and they reduce to the Legendre polynomials \(P_l(\cos\theta)\). A ZH function is just a 1D polynomial wrapped around an axis:

\[ f(\theta) = \frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{l=0}^{N} (2l+1)\, c_l\, P_l(\cos\theta), \qquad P_0 = 1,\; P_1 = x,\; P_2 = \tfrac{3x^2 - 1}{2},\; P_3 = \tfrac{5x^3 - 3x}{2}. \]

Play with the coefficients of the first three degrees below. Try for example \((c_0, c_1, c_2, c_3) = (1,\ 0.7,\ 0.49,\ 0.343)\):

Zonal harmonic, 4 free coefficients

Blue regions are negative — unlike HG, a truncated ZH series can dip below zero. Changing the shared \(g\) anywhere resets these coefficients to \((1, g, g^2, g^3)\).

Notice anything? With coefficients \((1, 0.7, 0.49, 0.343)\) the shape is suspiciously similar to the HG lobe with \(g = 0.7\). That is no coincidence: \(0.49 = 0.7^2\) and \(0.343 = 0.7^3\). Indeed, the generating function of the Legendre polynomials gives

\[ \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} (2l+1)\, g^l\, P_l(x) = \frac{1 - g^2}{(1 + g^2 - 2gx)^{3/2}}, \]

which is exactly the HG numerator. Therefore HG in ZH form is simply

\[ p(\theta) = \frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty} (2l+1)\, g^l\, P_l(\cos\theta) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (c_0, c_1, c_2, c_3, \dots) = (1,\ g,\ g^2,\ g^3,\ \dots). \]

The coefficients are just the powers of \(g\)! Truncating after \(l = 3\) already gives a very usable approximation — compare the 4-term ZH against the exact HG:

At high \(g\) the truncated series starts to ring — the blue halo is the Gibbs phenomenon. More bands fix it.

2.3 Rotating ZH into a direction

So far our lobe points along \(+z\). A phase function must follow the light direction \(\mathbf{d}\). Because a zonal harmonic depends only on the angle to its axis, rotating it is trivial — just evaluate the same 1D polynomial at the cosine with the new axis:

\[ f_{\mathbf{d}}(\omega) = \frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{l=0}^{N} (2l+1)\, c_l\, P_l(\omega \cdot \mathbf{d}). \]

If you need full SH coefficients of the rotated lobe (e.g. to store it next to your irradiance SH), the addition theorem gives them in closed form:

\[ c_l^m(\mathbf{d}) = c_l\, Y_l^m(\mathbf{d}), \]

i.e. the coefficient vector of band \(l\) is the power \(g^l\) times the SH basis evaluated at the direction. Try aiming the lobe around:

Rotated ZH lobe with HG coefficients

The arrow is the axis \(\mathbf{d}\). Note that the polar plot never changes: rotation preserves the shape, only the axis moves.

3. Conclusion

That's all, folks. The HG phase function is a zonal harmonic with coefficients \((1, g, g^2, g^3, \dots)\), and a zonal harmonic is rotated into any direction by a single dot product per band.

And if we wanted to integrate it against an irradiance (or radiance) environment map stored in SH, orthonormality collapses the integral into a plain dot product of the coefficient vectors:

\[ \int_{S^2} L(\omega)\, p(\omega)\, d\omega = \sum_{l,m} L_l^m\, p_l^m = \sum_{l=0}^{N} g^l \sum_{m=-l}^{l} L_l^m\, Y_l^m(\mathbf{d}). \]

Nine to sixteen multiplies for bands 2–3, no quadrature, no sampling — volumetric ambient lighting with an HG lobe for (almost) free.
